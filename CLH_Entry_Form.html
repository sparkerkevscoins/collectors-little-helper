How to Use This Form

Five steps — click any step to see the full instructions. New to this? Start by expanding them all.

Expand all steps ▼
1

Fill in this form

Describe your coin — gold fields are the must-haves

▼

Click the New Entry tab above. Fill in the gold-highlighted fields — those are the essential ones. Add as much extra detail as you know, but don't worry if you can't fill everything in; the AI will help with the gaps.

Adding several coins in one session? Click + Add Another Coin after filling in the first entry. A new collapsible panel appears for each additional coin — fill them all in, then click Generate Entry to produce a numbered batch output covering every coin at once. One paste to the AI, one response, one paste into your spreadsheet.

Tip: Even a rough description is enough to get started. You can always update entries later as you learn more about a coin.
⚠ Never clean your coins — even a gentle clean can scratch the surface and destroy numismatic value permanently. Store coins in capsules or 2×2 holders without cleaning them.
⚠ Watch out for coin roll scams — some sellers place a valuable coin on top of a roll of worthless ones. You have to destroy the roll to check the contents. Buy coin rolls only from the RAM or a well-known dealer.

Paste the entry — no typing required

One paste fills the entire row automatically

▼

The AI has formatted the entry as a single row — paste it straight into the spreadsheet and all the values land in the right columns automatically.

  • Scroll down to the first completely empty row below your existing entries
  • Click the first cell in that row — it's in the Item Type column (column B, just after the grey Item ID column)
  • Select the tab-separated row from the AI's response — click just before the first value and drag to the end
  • Copy it (Ctrl+C on Windows / Cmd+C on Mac)
  • Click back on your spreadsheet and press Ctrl+V (Windows) or Cmd+V (Mac)
  • The values flow automatically into separate columns — blue cells filled, grey cells (Item ID etc.) fill themselves
Works everywhere: This technique works identically in Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets, LibreOffice Calc, and Apple Numbers.
Save when you're done: Press Ctrl+S (Windows) or Cmd+S (Mac). If you store your file in Google Drive, upload the updated file so it's always backed up.
A note on values Every coin has up to three layers of value: its face value (what it says on the coin), its ingredient value (the metal content — a pre-1966 Australian 50c contains silver worth more than 50c regardless of condition), and its numismatic / market value (what collectors will actually pay, driven by rarity and demand). Estimated values in your catalogue reflect market value where researched — for personal reference only, not a formal appraisal. For insurance or estate settlement, always seek an independent valuation from a qualified numismatist.
Glossary of Terms
COIN TYPES & FORMATS
CirculatingA coin made for everyday use — spent as currency, not produced for collectors.
ProofThe highest manufacturing standard — struck using a special high-pressure process on polished dies, producing mirror-like fields and frosted design detail. In this system, Proof appears as the top option in the Grade field (which combines finish and condition).
UNC / UncirculatedA coin that has never been spent and shows no wear. The Grade field uses the full trade grading scale: FDC/BU (finest) → Gem UNC → Choice UNC → UNC → aUNC, then down through EF, VF, F, VG, G. Always write "Uncirculated" in full in titles — never UNC or Unc.
BU / FDCBrilliant Uncirculated / Fleur de Coin — the finest uncirculated standard, used by UK and European mints. Appears in the Grade dropdown as FDC/BU.
PNCPhilatelic Numismatic Cover — a coin presented with a commemorative stamp on an illustrated envelope or folder. Issued by Australia Post / RAM jointly.
PMCPhilatelic Medallion Cover — similar to a PNC but contains a medallion rather than a legal-tender coin. The medallion is not currency.
NIFC / NCLTNot Intended For Circulation / Non-Circulating Legal Tender — two terms for the same thing: a coin that is legal currency but was never meant to be spent. Produced solely for collectors. You may see either abbreviation depending on the issuer.
SetA group of coins issued and packaged together — e.g. a Proof Year Set containing one of each denomination for a given year.
METALS & COMPOSITION
AlBrAluminium Bronze — the gold-coloured alloy used in Australian $1 and $2 coins since 1984.
Cupro-nickelAn alloy of copper and nickel — used in Australian 5c, 10c, 20c, and 50c coins. Silver-coloured but contains no silver.
SilverUsed in premium collector coins in several purities — always check the specification:
.999 (Fine Silver) — 99.9% pure; most RAM/Perth Mint proof coins
.925 (Sterling) — 92.5% silver, 7.5% copper; common in UK and some Commonwealth issues
.900 — 90% silver; used in some older US and world coins
(St.) — Sterling, same as .925, used as an abbreviation in some catalogues
Cupro-nickel coins are silver-coloured but contain no silver — check the metal field carefully.
BronzeThe copper-based alloy used in pre-decimal Australian pennies and halfpennies, and 1c/2c decimal coins.
TREATMENTS & SPECIAL FINISHES
ColouredA coin with colour applied to part of the design — usually by pad printing or enamel. Common on commemorative $2 coins (e.g. Red Poppy).
FrostedA coin where the raised design elements have a matte "sandblasted" look against a mirrored background. Standard on most Proof coins.
GildedSelectively gold-plated — part of the coin's design has a thin layer of gold applied over the base metal or silver.
MintmarkA letter or symbol on a coin identifying where it was struck. Common Australian and Commonwealth mintmarks:
C — Canberra (Royal Australian Mint) — used on RAM collector releases
M — Melbourne (former Melbourne Mint, now closed; also used on some modern issues)
S — Sydney (former Sydney Mint, now closed; pre-decimal era)
B — Bombay (India) — used on some WWII-era Australian pennies struck in India
P — Perth (Perth Mint) — used on Perth Mint collector releases and some circulating coins
Circulating coins generally carry no mintmark; collector releases often do to distinguish them.
Privy markA small additional symbol added to a coin to denote a special or limited edition, coin fair exclusive, or commemorative variant — distinct from the standard mintmark. Examples: a small anchor for a naval theme, a tiny crown for a royal event, or an envelope symbol for a coin fair release.
DomedA coin with a curved (convex/concave) shape rather than flat — used on premium collector issues for visual effect.
CATALOGUE FIELD TERMS
MintageThe total number of coins struck (produced). Lower mintage generally means rarer and more valuable. Note: declared mintage caps may not equal actual coins produced.
CoACertificate of Authenticity — a numbered certificate included with collector coins confirming their authenticity and edition number.
EffigyThe portrait of the monarch on the obverse (heads side) of a Commonwealth coin. Australian coins have used six portrait generations since 1953.
ObverseThe "heads" side of a coin — typically carries the monarch's portrait on Commonwealth coins.
ReverseThe "tails" side — carries the main design, denomination, and country name.
FPSFrom Proof Set — indicates the coin was originally issued as part of a proof year set rather than as a standalone release.
RAMRoyal Australian Mint — Australia's official coin producer, based in Canberra. Produces all circulating Australian coinage and many collector releases.
DenominationThe face value of the coin — e.g. $2, 50c. In this catalogue, enter as a number: 2 for $2, 0.5 for 50c, 0.2 for 20c.
Types of ValueEvery coin has up to three layers of value:
Face value — the denomination printed on the coin; the minimum it is worth as currency.
Ingredient value — the value of the metal content. A pre-1966 Australian 50c contains silver worth significantly more than 50c at today's silver prices, regardless of numismatic condition.
Numismatic / market value — what a collector will actually pay, determined by rarity, condition, and demand. The most complex and variable of the three.
Types of ValueEvery coin has up to three layers of value:
Face value — the denomination printed on the coin; the minimum it is worth as currency.
Ingredient value — the value of the metal content. A pre-1966 Australian 50c contains silver worth significantly more than 50c at today's silver prices, regardless of its numismatic condition.
Numismatic / market value — what a collector will actually pay, determined by rarity (mintage), condition, and demand. This is the most complex and variable of the three.
GradeA standardised assessment of a coin's condition. Common grades: Proof, UNC (Uncirculated), aUNC (about Uncirculated), EF (Extremely Fine), VF (Very Fine), F (Fine).
ReedingThe milled edge on a coin — the ridges around the rim. "Interrupted reeding" means the ridges are broken by smooth sections, used as a security feature on some Australian $1 coins.
STORAGE, GRADING & CONDITION
Slab / SlabbedA coin that has been professionally graded and sealed in a tamper-evident hard plastic case by a grading service (e.g. PCGS, NGC). The grade is printed on the label. Slabbing protects the coin and provides third-party authentication.
Raw / LooseA coin that has not been professionally graded or slabbed — stored loose or in a basic holder. Most collector coins are raw. Also called "raw" when removed from original packaging.
CapsuleA round or rectangular clear plastic holder that snaps shut around a coin, protecting it from handling and environmental damage without touching the coin surface. The preferred storage for most collector coins.
2×2A 2-inch × 2-inch cardboard and mylar coin holder — two pieces stapled together around a coin. Inexpensive and widely used for circulating and lower-value coins. Not recommended for silver coins (the cardboard can cause toning).
Grade / GradingA standardised assessment of a coin's condition. Common grades from highest to lowest:
Proof — specialist manufacture; not a circulated grade
MS/UNC — Mint State / Uncirculated; no wear
aUNC — About Uncirculated; slight wear on high points only
EF / XF — Extremely Fine; light wear, all detail sharp
VF — Very Fine; moderate wear, major detail clear
F — Fine; even wear, design clear but flat
VG / G / AG — Very Good / Good / About Good; heavy wear
Coin Roll ScamA known fraud where a valuable-looking coin is placed on top of a roll of ordinary or worthless coins. The roll must be destroyed to verify the contents. Buy coin rolls only from the Royal Australian Mint directly, or from a trusted dealer. Never buy an "uncirculated roll" you cannot inspect.
ToningNatural discolouration of a coin's surface over time, caused by chemical reactions with the environment. On silver coins, light toning is often considered desirable ("patina"); heavy or uneven toning reduces value. Never try to remove toning — cleaning is worse.
PCGS / NGCProfessional Coin Grading Service / Numismatic Guaranty Corporation — the two most widely recognised third-party coin grading companies. A PCGS or NGC grade on a slab adds confidence and often a premium to a coin's value.
Album / FolderA purpose-made binder or folder designed to hold and display coins in individual pockets. Lighthouse and Dansco are popular brands. Use archival-quality (PVC-free) pages to avoid chemical damage to coins.
PRE-DECIMAL TERMS (Australian coins before 1966)
Penny / HalfpennyPre-decimal copper coins. 12 pennies = 1 shilling. The penny featured the kangaroo reverse.
ThreepencePre-decimal silver coin worth 3 pence — small, twelve-sided (dodecagonal) in later years.
SixpencePre-decimal silver coin worth 6 pence — featured the Australian Coat of Arms.
ShillingPre-decimal silver coin worth 12 pence — featured the Australian Merino ram.
FlorinPre-decimal silver coin worth 2 shillings (24 pence) — featured the Australian Coat of Arms.
CrownPre-decimal silver coin worth 5 shillings — large and relatively rare in Australian issues.
Trusted Research Sources

Ranked by reliability — primary official sources first, then community-verified databases, then real-world market data. Always prefer a higher-ranked source when sources conflict.

TIER 1 — OFFICIAL MINT SOURCES (most authoritative)
  • 1
    ramint.gov.au — Royal Australian Mint
    Official product descriptions, mintage data, and annual reports. The primary source for Australian decimal commemorative coins. RAM Annual Report appendices carry the definitive production figures.
  • 2
    perthmint.com — Perth Mint
    Official product descriptions and mintage data for Perth Mint issues — a large and distinct catalogue from RAM, covering gold, silver, and platinum collector coins.
  • 3
    royalmint.com — The Royal Mint (UK)
    Official source for British and Commonwealth collector coins. Useful for UK £5 commemoratives, Remembrance Day issues, and any coin bearing the British royal arms.
TIER 2 — COMMUNITY-VERIFIED DATABASES
  • 4
    numista.com — Numista
    The most comprehensive community-built coin database in the world. Excellent for mintage cross-referencing, design details, and world coins. Generally reliable but community-sourced — cross-check key figures against official mint sources.
  • 5
    renniks.com.au — Renniks Australian Coin & Banknote Values
    The standard printed catalogue for Australian coins — updated annually. Useful for pre-decimal and early decimal valuations. Values are catalogue estimates, not sold prices — treat as a guide rather than market reality.
TIER 3 — REAL-WORLD MARKET PRICES (actual sold values)
  • 6
    ebay.com.au — eBay Australia (Sold listings only)
    The most useful source for current real-world market values on Australian commemoratives. Search for your coin, then filter to Sold Items — this shows what buyers actually paid, not what sellers are asking. Asking prices alone are unreliable.
    ⚠ Always filter to Sold listings — asking prices can be wildly inflated.
  • 7
    coinarchives.com — CoinArchives
    Aggregates confirmed hammer prices from major international auction houses. Best for pre-decimal Australian coins, world coins, and rare items that trade at auction rather than on eBay. Less useful for modern RAM/Perth Mint commemoratives.
TIER 4 — SPECIALIST DEALERS (asking prices — use for reference only)
  • 8
    sterlingandcurrency.com.au — Sterling & Currency
    Sydney-based specialist in Australian commemorative and military coins. Good for gauging asking prices on PNCs, C-mintmark releases, and Remembrance Day series. Prices are retail asking, not sold comps.
  • 9
    ensleighcoins.com.au — Ensleigh Coins
    Australian dealer with a good range of Australian decimal and pre-decimal material. Useful for checking availability and general price range on common to mid-tier collector coins.
  • 10
    mycollectables.com.au — My Collectables
    Australian marketplace for collector coins and memorabilia. Useful for getting a sense of retail asking prices, particularly for RAM and Australia Post products.
TIER 5 — COMMUNITY EDUCATION & GENERAL REFERENCE
  • 11
    australiacoins.org — AustraliaCoins.org (Damian Walker)
    Beginner-friendly educational resource covering how to identify, condition-assess, and value Australian coins. Useful starting point for new collectors, particularly the three-types-of-value framework (face / ingredient / numismatic) and the error coins reference. Not a primary source for mintage data or market prices — use Tier 1–3 for those.
A note on valuations For insurance or estate settlement purposes, rely on Tier 1–3 sources and get an independent appraisal from a qualified numismatist rather than relying solely on catalogue or asking-price data. Dealer asking prices (Tier 4) can be significantly higher than actual market value. Be cautious of "free valuations" from dealers — their interest in buying your coin creates an inherent conflict.
